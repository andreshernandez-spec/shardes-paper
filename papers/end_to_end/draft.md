# Evolution Strategies Against Reinforcement Learning at Matched Data: A Measured Account on Tulu 3.1

## Abstract

Evolution strategies (ES) fine-tune language models without backpropagation and have
matched reinforcement learning (RL) on single tasks such as Countdown. We ask whether
they do so on a realistic post-training stream. Starting from Llama-3.1-Tulu-3-8B-DPO, we
run ES on exactly the prompts and the rollout count of the Tulu 3.1 GRPO run, score both
on held-out prompts, and measure why the two differ. At the reference ES configuration
(16 members, sigma 5e-4) the model ends 0.25 reward points below its start after the data
of 480 RL steps, where the released RL checkpoint is 1.9 above. The cause is not the
estimate's direction: projected onto the exact GRPO gradient of the same batch, the ES
update has 12% of the largest component a zeroth-order estimate can have, its expected
direction is at least as steep as the gradient, and a perfect estimate of RL's gradient
would gain 0.8 of what the update already gains per unit. The cause is length. The useful
part of an update is at most `alpha` = 5e-4 long beside a random part of
`alpha sqrt(d / N)`, 11 at 16 members, whose cost in reward equals the useful gain. On
Countdown the same model's perturbations carry 75 times more signal per prompt, because
the improvement is one behaviour every prompt shares. With 1,024 low-rank members the
useful gain per update is unchanged and the random part's cost falls as 1/N, so one update
nets about twice RL's gain on the same prompts at 64 times the rollouts, eight updates
accumulate at half that rate, and the random part's cost concentrates on the reasoning
tasks: instruction following rises at RL's own pace while MATH falls. Per unit of held-out
gain ES used 6.6 times RL's GPU-seconds on one update and about 15 over eight.

## 1. Introduction

Evolution strategies perturb a model's weights at random, score each perturbed copy, and
move the weights toward the copies that scored well. They need no gradient and therefore
no optimizer state, no reference model and no backward pass, and their copies communicate
only their scores. Qiu et al. (2025) reported that ES with a few dozen members matches or
exceeds GRPO on Countdown and on conciseness with models up to 8B, and EGGROLL (Sarkar et
al., 2025) made populations of thousands affordable with low-rank perturbations. Whether
ES matches RL where RL is used in practice, on a stream of mixed verifiable tasks from a
strong instruction-tuned start, has not been measured.

This paper measures it on Tulu 3.1 (Lambert et al., 2024): the same start checkpoint, the
same prompts in the same order, the same number of generated answers, scored the same
way on prompts neither method trained on, with the RL side taken from the released
intermediate checkpoints so that the baseline is somebody else's tuned run. The question
is whether ES learns the stream, and when it does not, which quantity separates it from
RL. The answer, in one sentence: an ES update is a correct estimate of the gradient whose
useful component is at most `alpha` long beside a random component of length
`alpha sqrt(d / N)`, and on a stream whose remaining gains are many prompt-specific
behaviours, the random component's cost equals the useful gain at the reference
population, so only the number of rollouts, not the fitness or the step rule, changes the
outcome. Section 3 writes the model down; Sections 4 to 8 measure each of its terms.

The scope is one model and one stream, one seed per configuration, greedy decoding with
the run's own verifiers as the measure, and an inference engine holding bf16 weights.
Section 2 states it once.

## 2. Setup

**Model and data.** The start is `allenai/Llama-3.1-Tulu-3-8B-DPO`; the RL run is the
GRPO training of `allenai/Llama-3.1-Tulu-3.1-8B` (open-instruct at version 3f37c29), which
took 48 prompts and 16 samples per prompt per step from a stream of 29,689 verifiable
prompts (GSM8K, MATH and IFEval-style constraints) that we rebuilt from the released
training code and data. Its released intermediate checkpoints at steps 40 to 480 are
the RL side; no RL was trained here.

**Matching.** One ES iteration uses 16 members, each decoding the next 192 prompts of the
stream, the prompts of four RL steps, so an iteration generates exactly the 3,072 answers
those four steps generate. Iteration g sees RL steps 4g+1 to 4g+4.

**ES.** The reference configuration of Qiu et al.: full-weight Gaussian perturbations of
all 8.03 billion weights at sigma 5e-4 (each member 44.8 from the start in Euclidean
length), fitness the member's mean reward over its prompts under greedy decoding, the
update `u = (alpha / N) sum_m z_m E_m` with `z` the z-scored fitness, `E_m` the unit-scale
perturbation and `alpha` = 5e-4. Members and updates are computed by the shardes library's
seed-regenerated noise streams, one leaf at a time, and written into a vLLM engine that
generates; every write is checked against the library's bits. Later experiments use
rank-1 perturbations of the attention and MLP matrices in mirrored pairs, served as LoRA
adapters built from their seeds; Section 7 describes them.

**Measurement.** Reward is the run's verifiers' score, 10 for a pass and 0 otherwise.
Held-out prompts are those of RL steps 121 to 160 and 481 to 520 (1,920 each), which no
compared model trained on. Comparisons are paired over prompts; standard errors are over
prompts. Rewards are decoded greedily, and because the engine holds bf16 weights, a
perturbation's length is reported as what survived the rounding (its effective length)
where that differs from the nominal.

**Scope.** One model, one stream, one seed per configuration, the reported step sizes, and
the H200 engine. The RL run's wall clock is not measured; its compute is estimated from
per-token rates in Section 8.

## 3. The model

Write a member's fitness as its linear part plus the rest: `f(theta + sigma E) = f(theta)
+ sigma g . E + r(E)`, with `g` the gradient of the smoothed objective and `r` everything
not linear in `E`. With `rho` the correlation between the z-scored fitness and its linear
part, the update's expectation is

    E[u] = alpha rho g_hat,                                        (1)

a step of length at most `alpha` along the gradient's direction, and the rest of `u` is a
random vector of length `alpha sqrt(d / N)` that `u` cannot distinguish from it. To first
order the update gains `alpha rho D` per unit, `D` the reward's slope along `g_hat`; to
second order its random part costs `c alpha^2 d / N`, `c` the reward's curvature along
random directions. The best step is a multiple `lambda` of `u` with net
`lambda alpha rho D - lambda^2 c alpha^2 d / N`, largest at

    net* = (rho D)^2 N / (4 c d).                                  (2)

Three things follow and are measured below. The useful gain per unit does not depend on
N while the cost falls as 1/N, so the net grows in proportion to N (Section 7). The step
`lambda` cannot help: it scales both parts (Section 5). And `rho` and `D` are the only
terms a fitness or an optimizer can touch, so measuring them against the exact gradient
bounds what any estimator could gain (Section 6).

## 4. ES does not learn the stream at the reference configuration

| | held-out reward (RL steps 121 to 160) |
|---|---|
| DPO start | 5.43 |
| RL, steps 40 / 80 / 120 | 5.87 / 6.40 / 6.69 (+1.26 at 120, mostly IFEval) |
| ES, 30 iterations (the data of RL steps 1 to 120) | 5.42 |
| random-fitness control, 30 iterations | 5.39 |

Thirty iterations at sigma 5e-4, 1e-3 and 2e-3 give no detectable change against a
control whose members receive random fitness (the largest slope 1.7 standard errors from
zero). Continued to 120 iterations, the data of RL steps 1 to 480, the sigma-5e-4 run ends
0.26 +- 0.09 above the control and 0.25 +- 0.09 below its own start (5.255 against 5.505
on RL steps 481 to 520), where the released step-480 checkpoint scores 7.37. The backend
reproduces the library bit for bit and learns Countdown faster than the reference
implementation at identical settings, so the null is not an implementation fault.

The RL run moves the weights little: the step-120 checkpoint is 0.476 from the start,
step 480 is 1.134, against 61 for ES after the data of 120 steps and 45 for a single
member's perturbation. Along RL's displacement the held-out reward rises +0.85 at
effective length 0.32 and +1.02 at 0.48; along random directions of the same lengths it
does not move (six changes within 1.3 standard errors of zero). RL's direction keeps: the
cosine between its moves to step 120 and to step 240 is 0.51, while consecutive ES updates
are independent random directions.

## 5. One update gains what its random part costs

Applying the first update both ways, `theta_0 +- lambda u`, on the 3,840 held-out prompts
separates its parts: half the difference of the two is the first-order gain (the odd
part), and their mean minus the start is the random part's cost (the even part), which
depends on the update's length and not its direction.

| per unit update, 16 members | gain | cost per unit squared |
|---|---|---|
| ranking on 768 prompts | +0.004 +- 0.003 (at most 0.010) | -0.0042 +- 0.0005 |
| expected from Eq. (1), (2) with measured terms | | -0.0038 |

Random weightings of the same 16 directions give gains of +-0.011, so the ranked update is
not distinguishable from one. At the run's step the net is about nothing, as the run
showed. Mirrored members (each perturbation and its negative) explain the gap to the
members' own fitness spread: two thirds of what makes a member score well is the same
whichever sign its perturbation has, and a weighted sum of perturbations does not inherit
that part.

## 6. The estimate is right; the useful step is short

**Against the exact gradient.** We computed GRPO's gradient at the start on the run's
batch for the first 192 prompts (16 samples each, the run's advantages and loss; 91 of
192 groups have unequal rewards and the rest contribute nothing to RL's step either) and
projected the measured updates onto it. The projection `u . g_hat / alpha` is `rho` times
the cosine between the estimator's own gradient and RL's; a ranking with no signal gives
noise of standard deviation `1 / sqrt(N)`.

| update | projection toward reward (noise sd) |
|---|---|
| 1,024 low-rank members | +0.120 (0.031) |
| 512 low-rank members, two independent decodes | +0.123, +0.116 (0.044) |
| 16 dense members | unresolvable (0.25) |

**The slope along RL's gradient.** The gradient is concentrated: 993 of 7 billion
elements carry 24% of its squared norm. Along it the held-out reward rises 7.3 +- 1.3
points per unit length at the smallest length the rounding allows (0.022), and the model
is broken by length 0.08. Along the unit sign vector, the direction of Adam's first step,
the slope is 0.76 +- 0.06 and holds to length 0.6: Adam's normalization is what makes
RL's gradient usable, a tenth as steep.

**What follows for ES.** Its useful component is at most `alpha` long per unit and
delivers 0.0047 +- 0.0004 at 1,024 members (Section 7), so the slope along its expected
direction is at least 9.4 per unit length, and the direction holds: the gain was linear to
length 112. A perfect zeroth-order estimate of RL's gradient (`rho` = 1 along `-g_hat`)
would gain `alpha` x 7.3 = 0.0037 +- 0.0007 per unit, 0.8 +- 0.15 of what the update
already gains. No fitness, control variate or smooth objective can raise the gain per unit
by more than that. Nor can an optimizer: Adam's per-element normalization sees element
magnitudes that are 99.97% noise of equal size, momentum applies each random part once
in total, and a nonlinear reshaping of a random matrix with a tiny useful part does not
raise the useful fraction. One iteration learns N numbers about an 8-billion-dimensional
gradient.

**Why the signal is small on this stream.** The members' ranking is mostly a property of
the prompts drawn. At the reference configuration a member changes 9.8% of the greedy
outcomes, and the share of the variance of its fitness over 192 prompts that is its own
(the reliability) is 0.12 (90% interval 0.01 to 0.47); scoring 8 samples per prompt at
temperature 1.0 with common random numbers gives 0.11, and mirrored pairs are no better
per rollout. The outcomes change because a member's answer departs from the start's
within the first dozen tokens on 86 to 90% of prompts, at positions where the start's top
two tokens are within a few bf16 steps of each other, and continues as a fresh draw. But
removing the decoding entirely does not make the ranking reliable: scored by the
log-probability contrast of fixed right and wrong answers the start had sampled (exact to
the bit on a rescore), a member's effects on two prompts correlate at 0.0024, and 36% of
its ranking is common to prompts, half of that a length artifact. A random perturbation
moves each uncertain prompt's success probability by about 8 points, and its effects on
two prompts correlate at 0.0095 (0.004 over all prompts).

On Countdown the same measurement comes out differently. With the same start model and
the same 16 noise directions, the members' effects on two Countdown prompts correlate at
0.30, and the ranking over 200 prompts is 42 to 68% real; for the 0.5B model that learns
Countdown in 100 updates, 1.06 and 0.94. There a perturbation changes two thirds of the
outcomes, many of them the same way (the model writes the answer format or it does not).
The margin of implied gain over random-part cost was about 600 for that model, against
about 6 on Tulu at 16 members.

## 7. More members: the gain stays, the cost falls, and the cost lands on reasoning

Members as rank-1 perturbations `sigma a b^T` of every attention and MLP matrix, in
mirrored pairs, each served as a LoRA adapter that the engine builds in memory from its
seed, 64 adapters per batch on one copy of the base weights (an adapter changes
log-probabilities as the same perturbation merged into the weights does, correlation
0.997). 1,024 members decoded the first 192 prompts; the update from the first N of them
was measured as in Section 5.

| members | gain per unit | cost per unit squared | best net per update, Eq. (2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16, dense | +0.004 +- 0.003 | -0.0038 | 0.001 |
| 128 | +0.0037 +- 0.0011 | -0.00054 | 0.006 +- 0.004 |
| 512 | +0.0047 +- 0.0006 | -0.00015 | 0.036 +- 0.010 |
| 1,024 | +0.0047 +- 0.0004 | -0.000077 | 0.071 +- 0.016 |
| RL, per four steps (the same 192 prompts) | | | 0.040 |

The gain is 11 standard errors from zero at 1,024 members and does not depend on N; the
cost falls as 1/N and is 1.2 to 1.3 times a dense update's at the same length. One update
matches RL per prompt at 512 members and exceeds it at 1,024, at 32 and 64 times the
rollouts.

**Eight updates.** At 512 members and the best step of Eq. (2), along the stream:

| | held-out change | IFEval | MATH | GSM8K |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ES after 4 updates | +0.018 +- 0.055 | | | |
| ES after 8 updates (the data of RL steps 1 to 32) | +0.141 +- 0.061 | +0.47 (0.07) | -0.46 (0.15) | +0.10 (0.12) |
| RL at step 40, RL steps 121 to 160 | +0.44 | +0.81 | -0.05 | +0.20 |

The gains accumulate at half the one-update rate, in RL's direction, and MATH falls where
RL holds it level.

**The random part's cost is task-specific.** At 1,024 members and length 112, one step's
even part is -1.07 on MATH, -0.48 on GSM8K and -0.19 on IFEval. A step size chosen from
the pooled cost over-steps the reasoning tasks. Eight updates at 1,024 members, with a
step per matrix type set by a one-update measurement of each type's gain and cost (the
pooled best net was 2.4 times the uniform one in that measurement) and a screen that
decodes with the full population only the prompts on which 128 screening members did not
all score alike, gave held-out -0.21 +- 0.06 after four updates and -0.32 +- 0.07 after
eight: IFEval +0.65 +- 0.09, GSM8K -0.72 +- 0.14, MATH -1.81 +- 0.16. The useful part
delivered what its one-update measurement implied (IFEval at RL's own pace on these
prompts), and the random part, twice as long as at 512 members, lost the reasoning tasks
at a rate the pooled quadratic model did not contain. On this stream ES trades the skill
every prompt shares for the fragile ones, and a longer random part makes the trade worse.

## 8. Compute

On one H200, per 192 prompts: RL's four steps decode 3,072 answers (100 GPU-s at 10,600
tokens/s), take a gradient on them (538 GPU-s at 4,465 tokens/s for the forward and
backward pass with gradient checkpointing) and a reference forward (146 GPU-s), 784 GPU-s
for +0.040 on its first 120 steps. One ES update at 512 members decodes 98,304 answers,
4,744 GPU-s at 7,000 tokens/s with LoRA members (a factor 1.5 below the base model's rate),
for +0.037. Per unit of held-out gain ES used 6.6 times RL's GPU-seconds on one update and
about 15 over the eight updates of Section 7. The screen above spends 54 to 62% of the
rollouts for 98.7% of the outcome changes that rank the members; a length cap gives up at
least as large a share of those changes as of the tokens it saves; twice the adapters per
batch decodes 21% slower. These are single-GPU rates: an RL node adds communication and
idling between generation and training that ES's independent members do not, by amounts
not measured here.

## 9. Discussion

Both methods see the same prompts and generate the same answers, compared within each
prompt's group. RL turns each answer's relative reward into the weight change that makes
it more or less likely; its step is entirely useful. ES learns one number per member and
infers the direction from how random changes to every weight correlated with those
numbers. The inferred direction is right and steep; the step along it is `alpha rho`, at
most 5e-4, beside `alpha sqrt(d / N)` of random movement whose cost sets the step size.
Whether the trade is affordable is a property of the stream: on Countdown from a weak
start one shared behaviour gives each score a large common part and the random part costs
little because the model has little to lose; on Tulu after DPO the remaining gains are
many small behaviours that a perturbation touches one prompt at a time, and the random
part damages a model that is already good, most where its competence is most fragile.

What ES would need is rollouts: the best useful step grows in proportion to N, from 0.0005
per update at 16 members to 0.015 at 1,024, with RL's 0.045 at about 3,000 if the cost
kept falling as 1/N, and the task-specific cost caps the step below that. What ES keeps is
structural: inference-only memory, communication of scalars, and any model that can be
served. On this evidence those pay for a compute penalty of several times only where RL
cannot run.

**Limitations.** One seed per configuration; the RL side is the released checkpoints,
scored by us, with the data matching taken from the training command; greedy decoding
with the run's verifiers as the measure, on held-out prompts from the training
distribution; bf16 weights, so small perturbations partly round away; the exact gradient
is one batch's at the start with the run's token-mean loss, and RL's later steps carry
Adam's state and clipping; the first-order and quadratic models are fitted at the lengths
measured and extrapolated no further than stated.

## 10. Related work

Qiu et al. (2025) fine-tune models up to 8B with ES at 30 members and sigma 1e-3 and
report parity with or gains over GRPO on Countdown, conciseness, Sudoku, ARC and MATH with
a math-specialized model. EGGROLL (Sarkar et al., 2025) serves low-rank perturbations at
populations of 256 to 8,000 at near batch-inference speed. Hoy et al. (2026) find ES
matching GRPO in single-task accuracy with updates of far larger norm and broader
off-task drift; Abdi et al. (2026) find the same norm gap and faster forgetting. Our
measurements agree in kind and add the matched-data comparison on a mixed stream, the
per-term account of the update, and the projection on the exact gradient. The scaling of
zeroth-order optimization with the problem's effective dimension is the MeZO analysis of
Malladi et al. (2023). Filtering prompts whose samples all score alike is used in GRPO
training (Yu et al., 2025, DAPO); the screen of Section 7 is its ES counterpart.

## 11. Conclusion

On the Tulu 3.1 stream, from the same start and with the same answers, ES does not learn
at the reference configuration and RL does. The ES update estimates the gradient
correctly; it moves at most `alpha` along it beside `alpha sqrt(d / N)` of random
movement, and on a stream of many prompt-specific gains the random movement's cost equals
the useful gain at 16 members. Only rollouts change that, and when they do, the random
part's cost falls on the reasoning tasks first. The method works where one behaviour is
shared by every prompt; it is the stream, not the implementation, the fitness or the step
rule, that decides.
