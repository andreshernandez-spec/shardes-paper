\section{Is the cheaper update as good?}
\label{sec:quality}

A faster update is only worth having if it points in a useful direction. We check that
two ways: the cosine between one ES update and the exact gradient of a differentiable
loss, and training on a task scored by a non-differentiable reward.

\subsection{Alignment with the exact gradient}

On the synthetic block the cosine between the update and the exact squared-error
gradient grows as the square root of the population for every arm, with log-log slopes of
0.494--0.508 (Appendix~\ref{sec:alignment}). That exponent is the classical one for
Gaussian smoothing \citep{nesterov2017,duchi2015}, and it survives centered ranks. A
heuristic gives the constant too. Suppose the fitness is locally linear, and each
member's standardized projection $z$ onto the gradient is close to Gaussian. Then
centered ranks are close to $\Phi(z)-\tfrac12$, and for $N\ll P$ the cosine is about
$\sqrt{3/\pi}\,\sqrt{N/P}$, or $1/\sqrt2$ of that for mirrored pairs, which have $N/2$
independent directions. The rank does not appear, because low-rank noise
$AB^\top/\sqrt r$ has the same covariance as full-rank noise. The Gaussian assumption is
the weak point: a low-rank member's projection is a sum of products of Gaussians, close
to Gaussian when the gradient is spread over many entries and not when it sits on a few.
On the synthetic block every arm is within 0.91--1.02 of this constant, at every rank
(Figure~\ref{fig:f5}). With $P$ = 494M and $N$ = 30, $\sqrt{N/P}$ is
$2.5\times10^{-4}$, which is why the cosines below are of order $10^{-4}$: most of each
update is not along the gradient.

0.7--2.0, Table~\ref{tab:tb6-audit}). The mirrored arms
lie within 0.87--1.20 of the synthetic reference, and all but that rank-4 configuration
at 0.80--0.92 of the heuristic constant. Unpaired rank 1 is 1.36$\times$ mirrored rank 1
at $N{=}240$, close to the $\sqrt2$ that $N$ against $N/2$ directions gives, and
1.17$\times$ at $N{=}30$; mirroring halves the directions and in exchange halves the
stored factors.

The predictions we fixed before these runs used a different variable and missed. Each
arm was predicted from its own synthetic fit against $N/d_{\mathrm{samp}}$, where
$d_{\mathrm{samp}}$ counts the independent random numbers in one perturbation ($r(m+n)$
per matrix at rank $r$). That predicted full rank within 9\% but put the low-rank arms at
about twice their measured alignment, and a 0.53 correction measured at 0.5B and fixed
in advance for 1.5B still overshot there: the observations were 23--26\% below the
predictions (Table~\ref{tab:tb6-audit}). The miss is in the transfer. On the block the
arms coincide at equal $N/P$, so a fit read at $N/d_{\mathrm{samp}}$ carries over the
block's $P/d_{\mathrm{samp}}$, 256 for rank 1, to models where it is 638 (0.5B) and 1063
(1.5B); that alone overstates rank 1 by $\sqrt{638/256}\approx1.6$ at 0.5B, most of the
miss. $N/P$ describes these measurements better. We found that after seeing the data, so
the synthetic reference is a post hoc reading, reported beside the preregistered one.

\subsection{Training on a task}

We trained on Countdown, arithmetic puzzles in the style of TinyZero \citep{tinyzero2025},
with settings adapted from \citet{qiu2025}: their population of 30, noise scale
$\sigma{=}10^{-3}$ and learning rate, converted to Eq.~\ref{eq:update}; centered ranks
where they standardize rewards, which makes our steps about 3.5$\times$ smaller; and 8
puzzles per update where their protocol uses 200 samples
(Appendix~\ref{sec:countdown} has the full setup). Each arm runs 500 updates, 120,000
scored training completions, with three seeds, on one A100. A completion scores 1 if it
solves the puzzle and 0.1 if it is well formed but wrong. Held-out accuracy is measured
on 2000 disjoint puzzles by greedy decoding every 50 updates.

The base model solves 1.2\% of the held-out puzzles and formats 43\% of its answers.
Formatting is learned at once: every arm formats 98.5\% or more by the first evaluation,
so Figure~\ref{fig:f7} shows the solve rate, which carries everything that differs
between arms. Training raises it to a plateau, 6.0--6.2\% for full rank and ranks 1, 4
and 16 at the end, and 5.55\% with the embedding frozen (27\% of the parameters). The
approach differs. At the first evaluation, 12k samples in, full rank solves 4.4\% of the
puzzles and ranks 1, 4 and 16 solve 1.5--2.2\%, with the seed ranges apart; by 36k
samples every arm is at 5.3--5.9\%. With one evaluation on the rising part of the curve
we can say that full rank learns faster per sample early on, not by how much.

Per update, rank 1 is faster. As configured, a steady-state update took 4.44\,s at full
rank and 2.62\,s at rank 1, 1.69$\times$. Part of that is how the two arms evaluate: the
full-rank arm scored its members in chunks of five, and the low-rank arm scores the whole
population in one batch. With both batching every member, on one host, the times are
3.62\,s and 2.52\,s: rank 1 updates 1.44$\times$ faster for the same evaluation. Against
update time (Figure~\ref{fig:f7}, right) the early gap mostly closes: full rank solves
4.4\% after 212\,s of updates and 5.5\% after 434\,s, rank 1 5.3\% after 257\,s. The
evaluations are too sparse to say which reaches a given solve rate first, and that axis
uses the arms as configured, not with matched batching.

The task separates training from failure, not small differences in update quality. A
population-16 run, fixed in advance as a positive control because by the square-root law
it aligns 27\% worse per update, does not test that either: on about the same budget it
takes 940 updates (120,320 completions) to population 30's 500 and reaches the plateau
sooner (Figure~\ref{fig:f7c}). On one task at one model size with three seeds, no rank
breaks training or ends at a different accuracy.
