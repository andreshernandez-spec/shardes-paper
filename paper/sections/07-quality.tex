\section{Do cheaper perturbations remain useful?}
\label{sec:quality}

A faster generation does not necessarily mean faster learning. We make two checks:
one-update alignment with the gradient of a differentiable loss, and training on a
task with a non-differentiable reward. Within each experiment the perturbation variants
use the same population and objective; the two experiments use different objectives.

\subsection{Alignment with the descent direction}

\input{generated/tb6}

We measure $\cos(\Delta\theta,-\nabla L)$ for the next-token loss on a fixed prompt
batch, using five perturbation seeds per configuration. At 0.5B we compare full-rank
noise with ranks 1, 4 and 16; at 1.5B we check full rank against rank 1 (Table~\ref{tab:tb6}). Rank-1 median alignment is 0.92--0.98 times
the full-rank median across these checks: 2--8\% lower in every case, with overlapping
seed ranges. The other tested ranks have similar medians
at 0.5B, with substantial seed variation at the smaller population. The ranges in the
table make that variation explicit.

The cosines are small, of order $10^{-4}$. Each generation tests very few directions
in a space with hundreds of millions of parameters, so a useful update can still have
a small cosine with the gradient. A locally linear, Gaussian-projection approximation
predicts alignment proportional to $\sqrt{N/P}$. It describes the synthetic block well
(Figure~\ref{fig:f5}); applying it to low-rank noise also depends on how the gradient is
distributed across matrix directions. We did not measure that structure on Qwen.
Appendix~\ref{sec:alignment} gives the derivation and the unpaired-sampling comparison.

This is evidence about update direction at the tested settings. Cosine ignores update
magnitude and does not measure progress over repeated updates. Earlier predictions,
fixed before these experiments, overestimated low-rank alignment. The $N/P$ explanation
was developed afterward. Evaluated at Qwen's $N/P$, the full-rank synthetic reference
puts the measured mirrored medians at 0.87--0.99 times its prediction, except rank 4
at $N=30$, whose
median is 1.20 times the prediction and whose seed range is wide. Table~\ref{tab:tb6-audit} preserves both the original predictions
and the later reference.

\subsection{Training on Countdown}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{f7-e13-heldout.png}
  \caption{Countdown solve rate with Qwen2.5-0.5B, mean and min--max range over three
    seeds. Left: scored training completions; all variants use 240 per update. Right:
    cumulative steady-state update time with the training runs' original batching.
    Compilation and held-out evaluation are excluded. Full rank uses chunks of five
    candidates; low rank uses one batch. Markers are measured
    checkpoints; connecting lines do not establish when a solve rate was first reached.}
  \label{fig:f7}
\end{figure*}

We train Qwen2.5-0.5B on Countdown arithmetic puzzles in the style of TinyZero
\citep{tinyzero2025}. Each generation evaluates 30 candidates on the same eight puzzles,
drawn from a training pool of 256, giving 240 scored completions per update. A completion
scores 1 if it solves the puzzle, 0.1 if well formed but wrong, and 0 otherwise.
Each variant runs 500 updates with three seeds on one A100. Thus the budget of 120,000
completions counts repeated evaluations, not distinct puzzles. We measure greedy-decoding
accuracy on 2000 disjoint held-out puzzles every 50 updates. The setup adapts
\citet{qiu2025}, with different score normalization and puzzles per update; details are
in Appendix~\ref{sec:countdown}.

All tested ranks learn, but their early progress differs (Figure~\ref{fig:f7}). The
base model solves 1.2\% of held-out puzzles. At the first checkpoint, after 12,000
training completions, full rank solves 4.4\%, compared with 1.5--2.2\% for ranks 1, 4
and 16, with non-overlapping seed ranges. By 36,000 completions every variant reaches
5.3--5.9\%, and their final means are 6.0--6.2\%. Formatting exceeds 98.5\% for every
rank by the first checkpoint; the solve rate carries the remaining differences.

All twelve main runs use the same learning rate. Their Euclidean distance from the
initial weights is 4.47--4.62 after 50 updates and 11.25--11.75 after 500. This supports
comparable overall movement across ranks; it does not show that this learning rate is
optimal for each variant, or that individual update norms match.

The timing comparison includes a batching difference. Full-rank candidates were
evaluated in chunks of five and low-rank candidates in one batch. Their mean steady-state
update times were 4.44\,s and 2.62\,s for full rank and rank 1. The right panel uses
those runs, so its difference cannot be attributed to perturbation rank alone.
Compilation and held-out decoding are excluded. A clean training comparison with
matched evaluation batching remains unmeasured.

The observed final accuracies are similar, but this does not establish equivalent
learning efficiency. There is only one early checkpoint before the curves approach the
plateau, so we cannot reliably compare time to a target accuracy. The experiment uses
one task, one model size and three seeds. An additional population-16 experiment reaches
the plateau sooner per scored completion, but also takes more updates, so it does not
isolate the effect of per-update alignment. That experiment and the frozen-embedding
variant are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:countdown}.
