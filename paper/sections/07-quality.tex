\section{Is the cheaper update as good?}
\label{sec:quality}

A faster update is only worth having if it points in a useful direction. We check that
two ways: the cosine between one ES update and the exact gradient of a differentiable
loss, and training on a task scored by a non-differentiable reward.

\subsection{Alignment with the exact gradient}

On the synthetic block the cosine between the update and the exact squared-error
gradient grows as the square root of the population, for every arm: log-log slopes of
0.494--0.508 over 456 cells of 30 replicates each (Appendix~\ref{sec:alignment}). That
exponent is the classical one for Gaussian smoothing \citep{nesterov2017,duchi2015}, and
it survives centered ranks. The constant is expected too. For a locally linear fitness,
centered ranks are close to $\Phi(z)-\tfrac12$ of each member's standardized projection
$z$ onto the gradient, which gives $\cos\approx\sqrt{3/\pi}\,\sqrt{N/P}$ for $N\ll P$, and
$1/\sqrt2$ of that for mirrored pairs. Low-rank noise $AB^\top/\sqrt r$ has identity
covariance, so the rank does not enter. With $P$ = 494M and $N$ = 30, $\sqrt{N/P}$ is
$2.5\times10^{-4}$, which is why the cosines below are of order $10^{-4}$: each update is
mostly noise, and what training uses is the part along the gradient, which accumulates
over updates while the rest averages out.

Table~\ref{tab:tb6} measures the same cosine on Qwen2.5-0.5B against the exact gradient
of the teacher-forced NLL, five perturbation seeds per cell, and repeats cells on a second
prompt batch and on Qwen2.5-1.5B. At equal population the rank barely matters: rank 1
aligns 0.92--0.98 times as well as full rank at both model sizes and on both batches,
rank 16 0.97--1.05, and rank 4 1.04--1.31. Every arm lies within 0.87--1.20 of the
full-rank synthetic fit evaluated at the same $N/P$ (the \emph{law} column), and most
mirrored cells measure 0.80--0.92 of the no-fit constant above. Unpaired rank 1 is
1.36$\times$ mirrored rank 1 at $N{=}240$, close to the $\sqrt2$ that $N$ versus $N/2$
independent directions predict; mirroring halves the directions and in exchange halves
the stored factors.

The predictions we fixed before these runs used a different variable, and did worse. Each
arm was predicted from its own synthetic fit against $N/d_{\mathrm{samp}}$, where
$d_{\mathrm{samp}}$ counts the independent random numbers in one perturbation ($r(m+n)$
per matrix at rank $r$). That predicted full rank within 9\% but put the low-rank arms at
about twice their measured alignment (the \emph{preregistered} column), and a 0.53 correction
measured at 0.5B and fixed in advance for 1.5B missed there by 23--26\%.
$d_{\mathrm{samp}}$ counts draws, not how much of the gradient a rank-$r$ family can
reach, and the population alone predicts better. We found that after seeing the data, so
the \emph{law} column is a post hoc reading, reported beside the preregistered one.

\subsection{Training on a task}

We trained on Countdown, arithmetic puzzles in the style of TinyZero \citep{tinyzero2025},
with the published ES settings of \citet{qiu2025} (population 30, $\sigma{=}10^{-3}$,
learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$), except for 8 puzzles per update where their protocol
uses 200 samples: 500 updates, 120,000 scored training completions, three seeds per arm,
one A100 (Appendix~\ref{sec:countdown} has the full setup). A completion scores 1 if it
solves the puzzle and 0.1 if it is well formed but wrong. Held-out accuracy is measured on
2000 disjoint puzzles by greedy decoding every 50 updates.

Figure~\ref{fig:f7} splits the held-out reward into its two parts, which matters: the
base model solves 1.2\% of the puzzles and formats 43\% of its answers, and every arm
formats 99\% or more by the first evaluation. The rest of training raises the solve rate
to a plateau, 6.0--6.2\% for full rank and ranks 1, 4 and 16 at the end, and 5.6\% with
the embedding frozen (27\% of the parameters). The plateau is the same across ranks. The
approach is not: at the first evaluation, 12k samples in, full rank solves 4.4\% of the
puzzles and ranks 1, 4 and 16 solve 1.5--2.2\%, with the seed ranges apart; by 36k samples
every arm is at 5.3--5.9\%. With one evaluation on the rising part of the curve we can
say that full rank learns faster per sample early on, not by how much.

Per update, rank 1 is faster. As configured, a steady-state update took 4.44\,s at full
rank and 2.62\,s at rank 1, 1.69$\times$. Part of that is how the two arms evaluate: the
full-rank arm scored its members in chunks of five, and the low-rank arm scores the whole
population in one batch. With both batching every member, on one host, the times are
3.62\,s and 2.52\,s: rank 1 updates 1.44$\times$ faster for the same evaluation.

The task separates training from failure (in a stress test released with the code, an
arm at four times the noise scale stayed at the base model for 150 updates), not small
differences in update quality once the plateau is reached. A population-16 run, fixed in
advance as a positive control because it aligns 27\% worse per update, does not test
that either: at equal samples it takes 937 updates to population 30's 500 and reaches the
plateau sooner (Appendix~\ref{sec:countdown}). On one task at one model size with three
seeds, no rank breaks training or reaches a different accuracy.
