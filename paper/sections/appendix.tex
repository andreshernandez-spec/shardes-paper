
\section{Measurement details}
\label{sec:implementation}

\paragraph{Repeatability.} With the fitness scores held fixed, the updates computed for
Qwen2.5-0.5B on one and on eight A100s differ by a relative $\UpdateRelErr$, measured as
\[
  \frac{\lVert\Delta\theta_1-\Delta\theta_8\rVert_2}
       {\lVert\Delta\theta_8\rVert_2},
\]
where $\Delta\theta_D$ is the update computed on $D$ devices and the norms treat all
parameters as one vector. Only this aggregate was recorded; it does not bound the error in
each parameter array.

\paragraph{Score precision.} The library requires fitness scores in float32 or wider,
even when the model is evaluated in bfloat16, and rejects narrower ones. On the synthetic
block, casting 256 losses to bfloat16 left only two distinct values, too few to rank the
candidates.

\subsection{The cost of ranking}
\label{sec:barrier}

Centered ranks need every candidate's score, so each device gathers the scores and sorts the
same list; this step is the same under both placements and cancels in Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover}.
It was measured in isolation on the v5e over \RankBenchConfigs{} configurations. Each
configuration was timed \RankBenchRepeats{} times, each timing ran a chain of
\RankBenchChain{} ranking steps and was divided by the chain length, and the median is
reported. The ranking step takes at most \RankStepSmallMax\,$\mu$s for populations up to a
thousand and about \RankStepMid\,ms at 4096. Going from one to eight devices adds
\RankAddedSmall\,$\mu$s at populations up to a thousand and \RankAddedMax\,$\mu$s at
$N=2^{18}$, about half of which is the 1\,MiB score gather (about \RankGatherIsolated\,$\mu$s
measured in isolation). The sort itself is repeated on every device and does not get faster
with more of them: at $N=2^{18}$ it takes \RankSortMax\,ms, against about
\RankGatherIsolated\,$\mu$s for gathering the scores. In complete generations, turning rank
shaping on adds \RankContextSmall\,$\mu$s at $d=512$, $N=1024$ and \RankContextLarge\,$\mu$s
at $d=2048$, $N=256$, within the spread of the repeats.

\subsection{How the timing components are measured}
\label{sec:timemodel}

\input{generated/tb8}

The byte counts in Section~\ref{sec:placement} describe complete array sizes. The bytes
each device sends also depend on the collective algorithm. For example, a ring all-reduce
with equal-sized chunks sends $2(D-1)/D$ times the array size per device
\citep{thakur2005,patarasuk2009}.

Table~\ref{tab:tb8} comes from a benchmark that runs only the collective, as a chain of
dependent operations. Each chain length is timed \LadderRepeats{} times after warm-up and the
median is used; the increase in time per additional operation estimates the collective's cost
without the fixed launch and synchronization cost of the compiled call, which is
\LadderFloorGPU\,ms on the A100 and \LadderFloorTPU\,ms on the v5e. These times are measured
in isolation, not taken from complete generations.

The reconstruction terms of Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} are timed by
\texttt{contraction\_isolation.py} in three programs: replication's full reconstruction,
splitting's local reconstruction followed by its all-reduce, and the local reconstruction
alone. Like the collective benchmark, each is timed as a dependent chain, \ProbeRepeats{}
times per chain length, and the increase per step is used. The third program gives $C_B$,
and its difference from the second gives $T_{\mathrm{add}}$, the time the all-reduce adds;
$T_{\mathrm{add}}$ can include scheduling changes the all-reduce causes, so it is not the
all-reduce's own duration. The replicated timing includes replication's second coefficient
gather, which \texttt{timemodel.py} subtracts, using the separately measured gather, to obtain
$C_A$; Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} therefore counts the gather once.

Reconstruction does not divide ideally among devices. The ratio $C_A/(D\,C_B)$, which is 1
when splitting divides the work exactly $D$ ways, is \TmSplitFullGPU{} for full-rank noise and
\TmSplitLowGPU{} for rank 1 on the A100, and \TmSplitFullTPU{} and \TmSplitLowTPU{} on the
v5e. For low-rank noise at width 2048, $T_{\mathrm{add}}$ is \TmAddOverIsolatedGPU{} times the
isolated all-reduce time on the A100 and \TmAddOverIsolatedTPU{} times on the v5e. With these
measured terms, on one host with eight devices, the median absolute error of
Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} is \TmMedianErrGPU\,ms on the A100 and \TmMedianErrTPU\,ms on the v5e,
and the largest is \TmMaxErrGPU\,ms and \TmMaxErrTPU\,ms. Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} gives the
faster placement in \TmCorrectGPU{} of \TmConfigsGPU{} configurations on the A100 and
\TmCorrectTPU{} of \TmConfigsTPU{} on the v5e; in the \TmMisses{} it misses, the measured
difference is \TmMissGaps\,ms. Giving the faster placement does not mean estimating the
difference accurately. \texttt{timemodel.py} reports every configuration.

With its measured terms, Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} gives the faster placement in all
\ModelResolvedGPU{} resolved A100 and all \ModelResolvedTPU{} resolved v5e configurations. The
A100 has more because the mirrored full-rank variant was run only there and fewer of its
comparisons are unresolved. A simpler version, with $C_B=C_A/D$ and the isolated all-reduce
times, gives the faster placement in \IdealCorrectGPU{} of \TmConfigsGPU{} and
\IdealCorrectTPU{} of \TmConfigsTPU{} configurations and misses \IdealMissesGPU{} resolved A100
ones: mirrored rank 1 at $(d,N)=(512,256)$ and non-mirrored rank 1 at $(2048,256)$. For
low-rank noise at width 2048 on the A100, it gives $t_A-t_B$ between $\IdealLowRankLoGPU$ and
$\IdealLowRankHiGPU$\,ms, against $\MeasuredLowRankLoGPU$ to $\MeasuredLowRankHiGPU$\,ms
measured. Keeping $C_B=C_A/D$ but using $T_{\mathrm{add}}$ instead of the isolated time gives
\InContextCorrectGPU{} of \TmConfigsGPU{} and \InContextCorrectTPU{} of \TmConfigsTPU{}, with
no resolved misses. So $T_{\mathrm{add}}$ is what corrects the \IdealMissesGPU{} A100 cases,
while measuring $C_B$ does not help in the unresolved configurations.
\texttt{paper\_evidence.py} reproduces these comparisons and Table~\ref{tab:worked}.

\paragraph{Other placements.} Replication and splitting are not the only placements. In
EGGROLL's vLLM setup, one primary device reconstructs the whole update and broadcasts the
updated model \citep[Appendix L.2]{eggroll2025}. This avoids reconstructing the update on
every device, but the full reconstruction remains on the critical path, followed by a
model-sized broadcast. Under the accounting of Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover}, this placement is
expected to be slower than replication by the time of the broadcast. It was not measured.

\paragraph{Overlap.} Under the current execution schedule, overlapping the all-reduce of
one update with the evaluation of the next generation would evaluate that generation with
weights one update old, which changes the algorithm. Within a generation, the update can
be divided into parts, and the all-reduce of each part can start as soon as that part is
reconstructed. PyTorch's distributed data-parallel training does this with gradients
\citep{li2020ddp}. Assuming the measured component costs remain unchanged, ideal overlap
replaces $C_B+T_{\mathrm{add}}$ with $\max(C_B,T_{\mathrm{add}})$. Under this assumption,
replication remains \OverlapLead\,ms faster in every tested rank-1 configuration at width
2048, on both platforms. This is a bound within this model, computed by
\texttt{overlap\_bound.py}; overlap was not implemented.

\subsection{Host-boundary check of Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover}}
\label{sec:boundary-audit}

\input{generated/tb7-audit}

Table~\ref{tab:tb7-audit} gives the values of Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} that
Section~\ref{subsec:boundary} compares with the two-host measurements. The expected values
start from the earlier eight-device single-host timings and adjust for the change in
all-reduce cost. For sixteen devices, the additional reconstruction saving is estimated
using ideal $1/D$ scaling.

The break-even rate in Section~\ref{subsec:boundary} compares one host with eight devices
($1\times8$) and two hosts with four each ($2\times4$). Let $S=t_A-t_B$ be the time
splitting saves on one host, and $T_{\mathrm{intra}}$ the time of its all-reduce within
that host. Across two hosts, the all-reduce of the $4P$-byte update at an effective rate
$\beta$ takes $4P/\beta$ instead of $T_{\mathrm{intra}}$, where an effective rate is the
array size divided by the all-reduce time, not a line rate. Ignoring latency and holding
the reconstruction times fixed, splitting remains faster while
$4P/\beta<S+T_{\mathrm{intra}}$, that is, above $\beta_*=4P/(S+T_{\mathrm{intra}})$. For
seed at width 2048, $S$ is \HostSavingSmallN{} and \HostSavingLargeN\,ms at $N=128$ and
256 (the $1\times8$ column of Table~\ref{tab:tb7-audit}). $T_{\mathrm{intra}}$ for the
96\,MiB update is \HostIntra\,ms, from the all-reduce measured on the cluster's first
host. This gives the \HostBreakEvenSmallN{} and \HostBreakEvenLargeN\,GiB/s of
Section~\ref{subsec:boundary}. Taking $S$ from the extremes of the repeats instead of the
medians gives \HostBoundsSmallN{} and \HostBoundsLargeN\,GiB/s. With
Table~\ref{tab:tb8}'s isolated time (\HostIsolatedIntra\,ms) as $T_{\mathrm{intra}}$, the
break-even rates change by at most \HostIsolatedShift\,GiB/s.

\subsection{Scaling with model size}
\label{sec:size}

Writing the terms of Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} as functions of the parameter count $P$ shows
which quantities decide the placement for larger models. Suppose the leading cost of
low-rank reconstruction is $C_A=kPNr$, where $k$ is a time per operation that includes the
effect of mirrored sampling and the hardware's arithmetic efficiency, and splitting takes
$C_B=qC_A$, with
$q=1/D$ when the work divides exactly among $D$ devices. Write the all-reduce time as
$\alpha+4P/\beta$, with a fixed latency $\alpha$ and an effective rate $\beta$
(Appendix~\ref{sec:boundary-audit}). Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} then favors splitting when
\[
  (1-q)kNr > \frac{4}{\beta}
               +\frac{\alpha-T_{\mathrm{ag}}(4N)}{P}.
\]
If $k$, $q$ and $\beta$ do not change with $P$, the second term on the right vanishes as
$P$ grows, and the condition becomes $(1-q)kNr>4/\beta$: the product of population and
rank decides the placement, not the parameter count. For full-rank noise, $C_A=kPN$, and
the same argument applies without $r$.

In practice $k$, $q$ and $\beta$ are not constants. $k$ depends on matrix shapes and on
how the compiler combines operations; $q$ is above $1/D$ for low-rank noise on the block
(Appendix~\ref{sec:timemodel}); and $\beta$ depends on message size and on the connection
between devices. Generating the factors of each $m\times n$ matrix also takes time
proportional to $Nr(m+n)$. How this grows with the total parameter count depends on whether
the model gains wider matrices, more layers, or both. The constants measured on the block
therefore do not give a threshold for larger models.

\section{Real-model timing and memory}
\label{sec:memory}

\input{generated/tb5}

Table~\ref{tab:tb5} gives absolute generation times behind Figure~\ref{fig:f9}.
The grid has \QwenRecords{} records. For \QwenUnknownRecords{} of them the exact code version
cannot be confirmed, so they are excluded; the remaining \QwenKnownRecords{} include
\QwenKnownTimings{} timings and \QwenKnownOOM{} memory failures. Only configurations with two
such records, one per placement, enter a placement comparison, which leaves
\QwenLowComparisons{} low-rank and \QwenFullComparisons{} full-rank comparisons; every
eight-device record qualifies. Excluded comparisons are shown as dashes, distinct from observed
memory failures, and the excluded records remain in the experiment directory.

At eight devices, ranks 1, 4 and 16 run at every population up to \QwenLowFitMaxN{} and run
out of memory at \QwenLowOOMN{}, under both placements; seed, mirrored runs at all
\QwenPopulationCount{} populations. Across device counts, whether a low-rank configuration
fits depends only on the number of candidates per device: every configuration with
\QwenFitPerDevice{} or fewer fits, and every one with \QwenOOMPerDevice{} or more does not,
for all three ranks and both placements. Seed evaluates one candidate at a time on each
device, while the low-rank variants evaluate all of a device's candidates together against
the shared weights. Compiling one generation of a reduced model for the CPU and reading the
compiler's estimate of temporary memory (\texttt{e17\_memory\_probe.py}) shows the effect of
this difference. With candidates evaluated together, temporary memory grows in proportion to
the number of candidates per device and to the number of prompts, and it is no larger at
rank 16 than at rank 1. With seed's evaluation it does not grow with the candidates, and
evaluating seed's candidates together reproduces the growth. This points to the activations
of the candidates evaluated together, not the stored factors, as what exceeds the device
memory. The probe uses a reduced model on the CPU; it indicates the cause but does not
measure the limit for Qwen on the TPU.

\section{Scaling grids}
\label{sec:grids}

Figures~\ref{fig:m1-a100}--\ref{fig:m2-tpu} give the full strong- and weak-scaling grids,
with every perturbation variant under both placements. In strong scaling the total population
is fixed; in weak scaling the population per device is fixed. Each figure shows parallel
efficiency, the throughput per device relative to that at the series' own smallest measured
device count, so the ideal is 1 for every series. The strong-scaling figures also show time
per generation, and the weak-scaling figures peak memory per device.

In weak scaling at width 2048 with 32 candidates per device, the rank-1 variants keep an
efficiency of \ScalingRankOneGPU{} at eight devices on the A100 and \ScalingRankOneTPU{} on
the v5e. Under replication, every device reconstructs the update for the whole population,
which in weak scaling grows with the number of devices; with full-rank noise this
reconstruction is expensive, and dense and seed fall to \ScalingReplDenseGPU{} and
\ScalingReplSeedGPU{} on the A100 and to \ScalingReplDenseTPU{} and \ScalingReplSeedTPU{} on
the v5e. Under splitting the same two variants keep \ScalingSplitFull. In strong scaling on
the v5e, some efficiencies exceed 1: up to \ScalingStrongMax{} at width 512, for rank 1 at
$N=1024$ on two devices, and up to \ScalingStrongMaxWide{} at width 2048.

\section{Reproduction}
\label{sec:repro}

The figures, the tables and the numbers stated in the text are generated from the saved
results in \paperurl{}; the library is released at \librarycite{}. Each result record stores
the code version that produced it, the devices, and the versions of JAX and the other
software it ran with.

\texttt{make -C paper} runs the generators and builds the PDF; \texttt{paper/Makefile} lists
the commands, and \texttt{paper/README.md} explains how to select the Python environment. The
generators write the tables and the numbers the text states, as LaTeX macros in
\texttt{paper/generated}, and each stops if a record contradicts a statement the text makes in
words, such as a claim about every configuration. Generated files are not edited by hand. The
figures come from \texttt{plot\_paper.py} (block placement), \texttt{plot\_cost.py} (cost) and
\texttt{plot.py} (scaling grids) in \texttt{experiments/phase2}, and from
\texttt{plot\_e17.py} (real-model placement) in \texttt{experiments/countdown};
\texttt{make} runs all but \texttt{plot.py}.

\texttt{phase2/timemodel.py} reports Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} for every configuration,
\texttt{phase2/overlap\_bound.py} the overlap bound, and \texttt{phase2/comms.py} and
\texttt{phase2/tb2.py} the byte counts of the compiled programs. The score-precision count in
Appendix~\ref{sec:implementation} comes from \texttt{tools/bf16\_probe.py} in the library. The
library's test of agreement across device counts runs on the CPU with eight simulated devices.
An automated check verifies that regenerating the generated files changes no bytes and that
the code version each result record cites remains available.
