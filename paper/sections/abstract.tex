\begin{abstract}
Evolution strategies (ES) lend themselves well to distributed workloads because most of
the work is independent. However, the gradient estimation requires the bringing together
of information, and there are two main strategies to do this: exchange only scalar
fitness scores, but each device then reconstructs the same parameter update, or have
each device calculate partial updates and exchange those. The faster choice depends on
whether the computation saved outweighs the communication added. Using one JAX implementation,
we measure this tradeoff for full-rank and low-rank perturbations on A100 GPUs and TPU v5e
chips. On a transformer-block benchmark with eight devices in one host, splitting gives
1.5--5.6$\times$ speedups for the unpaired full-rank variants; for low-rank perturbations at the
larger matrix width, it takes 4--46\% longer than replication. The placement preference
carries over to Qwen2.5-0.5B on the TPU, while across a slow connection between two A100
hosts, replication wins for both perturbation types at the larger matrix width, reversing
the full-rank preference. In training experiments with Qwen2.5-0.5B, the tested
perturbations align similarly with the true gradient and reach similar final accuracy on
the Countdown arithmetic task, but full rank learns faster early on. Code, configurations,
and results are released.
\end{abstract}
