\section{Where to compute the sum}
\label{sec:placement}

Each of $D$ devices holds the full weights and evaluates $N/D$ members. After
evaluation every device has the fitnesses of its own members, and the sum of
Eq.~\ref{eq:update} has to be formed somewhere. We compare two places
(Figure~\ref{fig:placements}).

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Replicated (A).} Every device gathers all $N$ fitnesses and computes the
    whole sum itself, regenerating every member's perturbation. Only scalars move: two
    gathers of $4N$ bytes (the raw fitnesses, and the shaped weights, which the
    implementation gathers a second time). This is the arrangement behind ``ES only
    communicates scalars'' \citep{salimans2017}.
  \item \textbf{All-reduce (B).} Every device gathers the fitnesses, sums its own $N/D$
    members, and the partial sums are added with an all-reduce of a model-sized buffer:
    $4N$ plus $4P$ bytes. Each device does $1/D$ of the sum.
\end{itemize}
These byte counts are collective payloads, the size of the buffers the collectives are
called on. What crosses the wire depends on how the collective is implemented; a ring
all-reduce, for one, sends about twice its payload from every device.

Since evaluation and the update are shared, to a first approximation the two differ in
time per generation by the sum and the collectives:
\begin{equation}
  t_A - t_B \;\approx\; (C_A - C_B) \;+\; T_{\mathrm{ag}}(4N) \;-\; T_{\mathrm{ar}}(4P),
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
where $C_A$ is the time to form the whole sum on one device, $C_B$ the time for one
device's share of it, and $T_{\mathrm{ag}}$, $T_{\mathrm{ar}}$ the times of an
all-gather and an all-reduce of the given payloads. This is a timing model, not an
identity: it assumes the terms add, and that each costs inside a generation what it
costs when timed on its own. If the sum split perfectly, $C_B$ would be $C_A/D$ and the
saving $(1-1/D)\,C_A$. In words, \emph{splitting the sum helps when the computation it
saves takes longer than the communication it adds}. The gather costs microseconds
(Table~\ref{tab:tb8}), so B is faster roughly when $C_A - C_B > T_{\mathrm{ar}}(4P)$.

$T_{\mathrm{ar}}$ grows with the model and depends on the interconnect. $C_A$ depends on
the perturbation. For seed noise, forming the sum means regenerating $N$ full
perturbations, $NP$ random numbers, and adding them one member at a time; dense noise
reads $NP$ stored numbers. For rank-$r$ noise the sum over one weight matrix is a single
matrix multiply over factors already in memory,
\[
  \textstyle\sum_i w_i A_i B_i^\top = [w_1A_1,\dots,w_NA_N]\,[B_1,\dots,B_N]^\top ,
\]
and no perturbation matrix is formed. Each
perturbation has rank $r$, but their sum, the update, generally does not. So $C_A$ is
large for full-rank noise and small for low-rank noise, and the faster placement should
differ by perturbation, model size, device count and fabric.

\paragraph{What was fixed before measuring.} The byte counts and the direction were
committed before the single-node runs: B should win for dense and seed noise, A for
low-rank noise. (The counts were then corrected from the compiled programs, $4N$ to $8N$
for A and $4P$ to $4P+4N$ for B, which changes nothing.) Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} was
written after those runs, with its terms measured separately (Table~\ref{tab:tb8},
Appendix~\ref{sec:timemodel}), and used once as a prediction, for the host boundary
(Section~\ref{subsec:boundary}).

\paragraph{Other placements.} A and B are the two placements studied here, not the only
ones. A's second gather is an implementation choice; a hierarchical scheme could
replicate within a host and all-reduce across hosts; compressed collectives shrink B's
payload at some cost in precision. We did not measure these, and their run time need not
fall between A's and B's.

\paragraph{Overlap.} Hiding B's all-reduce behind the next generation's evaluation is not
free: that evaluation needs the updated weights, so it would run at weights one step
stale, which is a different algorithm. The overlap that keeps the algorithm hides the
all-reduce behind B's own local sum, as data-parallel training does behind the backward
pass, and at best replaces $C_B + T_{\mathrm{ar}}$ by $\max(C_B, T_{\mathrm{ar}})$. With
the measured $C_A$ and $C_B$ and the all-reduce timed inside the program, A stays ahead
by 0.40 to 1.98\,ms at every low-rank configuration at width 2048 on both platforms
(0.77 to 2.29\,ms without overlap). This is a bound from measured terms; we did not
build an overlapped implementation.
