\subsection{Placement on the block}

Figure~\ref{fig:f2} gives the answer on the block. At eight devices the all-reduce
placement is faster in every full-rank configuration: $t_B/t_A$ is 0.42--0.55 for dense
noise and 0.52--0.69 for seed noise on the A100 (0.70--0.83 for mirrored seed noise, run
on the A100 only), and 0.18--0.20 and 0.50--0.55 on the v5e, and the advantage grows
with every device added. Rank-1 noise goes the other way at width 2048: the all-reduce
placement takes 4--15\% longer on the A100 and 17--46\% longer on the v5e, paired or
unpaired. At width 512 rank 1 is near a tie ($t_B/t_A$ 0.92 to 1.10), close to the
spread the two placements show at one device.

Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} accounts for the pattern with its terms measured separately.
Table~\ref{tab:tb8} gives the collectives. At $d{=}2048$ B's all-reduce costs 0.93\,ms
on the A100 and 2.01\,ms on the v5e; at $d{=}512$, 0.12\,ms on both; the fitness gather
stays under 21\,$\mu$s even at $N{=}2^{18}$. In every full-rank configuration B's
all-reduce is 0.2--6.8\% of the time B saves, so what decides the full-rank side is the
sum, not the bytes. In the eleven configurations where A wins, all low-rank, the
all-reduce is 57--155\% of B's deficit, so the low-rank side is the collective and little
else. The platforms differ through the same term: the 96\,MiB all-reduce costs
2.17$\times$ more on the v5e, and B's deficit for rank 1 at width 2048 grows from 4--15\%
to 17--46\%. The bytes are identical on both.

With ideal splitting ($C_B = C_A/D$) and the collective timed in isolation, the model
misses two things, both measured. B's local share of a small sum takes much longer than
$C_A/D$: for rank 1 on the A100, $C_A/(D\,C_B)$ is 0.18--0.46, against 0.60--1.01 for
full-rank noise. And at width 2048 the same all-reduce runs 1.75--1.80$\times$ slower
inside the lean low-rank program than in isolation. With the measured $C_B$ and the
in-program all-reduce, the model gets the sign of 18 of 20 A100 configurations and 15 of
16 v5e configurations, and the three misses are near-ties at width 512 (within
0.02--0.09\,ms); Appendix~\ref{sec:timemodel} goes through them. That makes it a useful
account of the measurements, not an exact decision rule.

\subsection{Placement on a real model}

\input{generated/tb7}

On Qwen2.5-0.5B the signs carry over (Figure~\ref{fig:f9}). Three directional predictions
were fixed before the run, and all three hold. The full-rank arm favours the all-reduce
more with every device, down to $t_B/t_A = 0.69$--$0.73$ at $D{=}8$. The low-rank arms
favour replicating at 17 of the 18 shapes that fit, by 1.10--1.68$\times$, more than on
the block: the model is 494M parameters, so the all-reduce is large next to a small sum.
The eighteenth, rank 16 at $N{=}128$ and $D{=}8$, is a tie (1.01). The replicated
placement's lead shrinks as the rank rises, because the sum grows with $r$ while the
all-reduce does not: rank 16 is below rank 1 at every population they share (1.50
against 1.68 at $N{=}32$, 1.01 against 1.16 at $N{=}128$). It shrinks with $N$ for the
same reason.

In absolute terms the full-rank update falls from 4.9\,s to 673\,ms between one and
eight devices at $N{=}32$, and the low-rank arms take 71--272\,ms wherever they fit. They
fit less often: the low-rank arms evaluate each device's whole population in one batch,
which is where their speed comes from, and the batch's activations are what run out of
memory, not the perturbation factors (Appendix~\ref{sec:memory}). A wall-clock
comparison across arms therefore compares two ways of evaluating a population as well as
two kinds of noise.

\subsection{Across a host boundary}
\label{subsec:boundary}

% F4 -- experiments/phase2/figures/f4-cost-bfloat16.png (plot_cost.py)
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{f4-cost-bfloat16.png}
  \caption{Time per generation against the dense baseline at the same shape, one device,
    bfloat16. Blue: faster than dense; red: slower. Hatched: the dense baseline does not
    fit the device (``dense OOM''); blank: neither arm fits (``both OOM''). Ranks 4 and
    16 behave like rank 1 (Table~\ref{tab:tb3}).}
  \label{fig:f4}
\end{figure*}

A single host is where an all-reduce is cheapest. We repeated six configurations on two
8$\times$A100 nodes with both placement predictions fixed before the run. InfiniBand
carried no NCCL traffic on any of five clusters we drew, so the nodes talk over TCP
sockets; measured at the payload it moves, the all-reduce runs at 64\,GiB/s within a
node and 0.6--0.8\,GiB/s across the two (Table~\ref{tab:tb7}; Table~\ref{tab:tb8}'s
98\,GiB/s was measured inside a running program on another node).

Splitting the same eight devices across the boundary (\texttt{1x8} to \texttt{2x4})
adds 107--110\,ms to B's generation at width 2048, which is more than the 21--51\,ms B
was saving there. So the seed arm, which wins with the all-reduce on one node, loses with
it across two, at both populations and at sixteen devices. At width 512 the all-reduce is
6\,MiB and the seed arm keeps it. Rank 1 favoured replicating already and does so by more.
The dependence on the fabric is the $T_{\mathrm{ar}}$ term of Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover}.

The prediction we fixed before the \texttt{2x8} run got 4 of 6 signs and a penalty of
about 10\,ms instead of 110, because the calibration that fed it all-reduced $1/D$ of the
payload it was labelled with and so reported a bandwidth $D$ times too high (515 and
9.2\,GiB/s instead of 64 and 0.58). We found the error after the runs. Recomputed with
the bandwidth at the payload actually moved, the model matches every \texttt{2x4}
configuration within 6\,ms and gets all six \texttt{2x8} signs, but at \texttt{2x8} it
overestimates B's deficit by 46--63\,ms for the seed arm at width 2048.
Table~\ref{tab:tb7} shows the preregistered and the corrected numbers side by side; only
the first is a prediction made before measuring.

The fabric we measured is slow. If a cluster sustained an effective all-reduce bandwidth
of 12--50\,GiB/s on this payload, a 96\,MiB all-reduce would take 2--8\,ms, and by
Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} the seed arm would keep the all-reduce there. Those bandwidths
are an assumption, not a measurement of any cluster.

\subsection{What low-rank noise saves}

\input{generated/tb1}

EGGROLL motivates low-rank noise by what dense perturbations cost on GPUs, so the TPU,
whose matrix unit makes dense multiplies cheap, might narrow the gap. It widens it
(Figure~\ref{fig:f4}). A rank-1 generation costs 0.05--0.16$\times$ the dense one on the
v5e against 0.13--0.42$\times$ on the A100, and on the 16\,GB chip the dense baseline does
not fit over most of the grid. Seed regeneration is the price side of Qiu et al.'s
design: 5.1--5.9$\times$ the dense time on the v5e and 1.3--3.2$\times$ on the A100, for
the only arm that never runs out of memory. Whether that cost is random-number throughput
or the loss of the shared base multiply, we did not isolate.

Forcing full-precision multiplies costs the low-rank arms 1.6--1.9$\times$ and the others
at most 8\% (Table~\ref{tab:tb3}). Computing in bfloat16 rather than float32 is within
7\% on the v5e and on the A100 is 8--12\% \emph{slower} for dense and seed noise: the
datatype is a memory choice more than a speed one.

\subsection{Against the reference code}

On one device the rank-1 arm runs at 0.97--1.02$\times$ the throughput of EGGROLL's
released code on the A100 and 0.68--0.85$\times$ on the v5e (Table~\ref{tab:tb1}), a
general API against a specialized one; we did not isolate the TPU gap. The reference
implementations do not shard, so on eight devices the library reaches 1.3--3.4$\times$
the EGGROLL code's throughput on the A100 and 1.7--6.1$\times$ on the v5e. The seed arm
runs at 0.13--0.77$\times$ the naive baseline at these shapes: regeneration costs
throughput, and what it buys is memory. evosax does not fit $d{=}2048$ on the 16\,GB chip.
The reference's rank-1 path also fails to compile on that chip at $d{=}512$,
$N{=}16384$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:refimpl}). We did not benchmark Qiu et al.'s released code.
