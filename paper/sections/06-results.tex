\subsection{Placement on the block}

% F2b -- experiments/phase2/figures/f2b-crossover-vs-d.png (plot_paper.py)
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{f2b-crossover-vs-d.png}
  \caption{Where to compute the sum, on the block benchmark: $t_B/t_A$, the time of a
    generation with the all-reduce placement over the time with the replicated one, at
    every cell of the strong-scaling sweep (solid $d{=}2048$, dashed $d{=}512$; two
    populations per width). Below 1 the all-reduce is faster. At $D{=}1$ the two
    placements are the same program. Full-rank arms (dense, seed) fall further below 1

    within a few percent of 1 at $d{=}512$.}
  \label{fig:f2}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:f2} gives the answer on the block. At eight devices the all-reduce
placement is faster in every full-rank cell: $t_B/t_A$ is 0.42--0.55 for dense noise and
0.52--0.69 for seed noise on the A100, and 0.18--0.20 and 0.50--0.55 on the v5e, and the
advantage grows with every device added. Rank-1 noise goes the other way at width 2048:
replicating the sum is faster by 4--15\% on the A100 and 17--46\% on the v5e. At width
512 the two placements are within a few percent for rank 1 ($t_B/t_A$ 0.92 to 1.10).
At width 2048 pairing does not change the sign: paired and unpaired rank 1 both favour
replicating.

\input{generated/tb8}

Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} explains the pattern with the two terms measured separately.
Table~\ref{tab:tb8} gives the collectives. At $d{=}2048$ B's all-reduce costs 0.93\,ms
on the A100 and 2.01\,ms on the v5e; at $d{=}512$, 0.12\,ms on both; the fitness gather
stays under 21\,$\mu$s even at $N{=}2^{18}$. In every cell where B wins, its all-reduce
is 0.7--3.6\% of the time B saves, so what decides the full-rank side is the sum, not the
bytes. In every cell where A wins, the all-reduce is 57--155\% of B's deficit, so the
low-rank side is the collective and little else. The platforms differ through the same
term: the 96\,MiB all-reduce costs 2.17$\times$ more on the v5e, and A's margin at width
2048 grows from 4--15\% to 17--46\%. The bytes are identical on both.

The simple form of the equation misses two things, both measured: B's local share of a
small sum does not shrink to $C/D$ (for rank 1 it reaches 0.18--0.46 of the ideal saving on
the A100, against 0.60--1.01 for full-rank noise), and the same all-reduce runs
1.75--1.80$\times$ slower inside the lean low-rank program than in isolation. With both
included, the equation gets the sign of 18 of 20 A100 cells and 15 of 16 v5e cells;
Appendix~\ref{sec:timemodel} goes through them.

\subsection{Placement on a real model}

% F9 -- experiments/countdown/figures/f9-e17-crossover.png (plot_e17.py)
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{f9-e17-crossover.png}
  \caption{The same question on Qwen2.5-0.5B: $t_B/t_A$ for one full update (ask,
    teacher-forced NLL, tell) on the TPU v5e-8, over four arms, four populations and
    $D\in\{1,2,4,8\}$. Below 1 the all-reduce is faster. Missing points did not fit the
    16\,GB chips under either placement; the absolute times are in
    Table~\ref{tab:tb5}.}
  \label{fig:f9}
\end{figure*}

On Qwen2.5-0.5B the signs carry over (Figure~\ref{fig:f9}). Three directional predictions
were fixed before the run, and all three hold. The full-rank arm favours the all-reduce
more with every device, down to $t_B/t_A = 0.69$--$0.73$ at $D{=}8$. The low-rank arms
favour replicating at all 18 shapes that fit, by up to 1.68$\times$, more than on the
block: the model is 494M parameters, so the all-reduce is large next to a small sum. And
A's margin shrinks as the rank rises, because the sum grows with $r$ while the all-reduce
does not: rank 16 is below rank 1 at every population they share (1.50 against 1.68 at
$N{=}32$, 1.01 against 1.16 at $N{=}128$). It shrinks with $N$ for the same reason.

In absolute terms the full-rank update falls from 4.9\,s to 673\,ms between one and
eight devices at $N{=}32$, and the low-rank arms take 71--272\,ms wherever they fit. They
fit less often: the low-rank arms evaluate each device's whole population in one batch,
which is where their speed comes from, and the batch's activations are what run out of
memory, not the perturbation factors (Appendix~\ref{sec:memory}). A wall-clock
comparison across arms therefore compares two ways of evaluating a population as well as
two kinds of noise.

\subsection{Across a host boundary}
\label{subsec:boundary}

\input{generated/tb7}

A single host is where an all-reduce is cheapest. We repeated six cells on two
8$\times$A100 nodes with both placement predictions fixed before the run. InfiniBand
carried no NCCL traffic on any of five clusters we drew, so the nodes talk over TCP
sockets; measured at the payload it moves, the all-reduce runs at 64\,GiB/s within a
node and 0.6--0.8\,GiB/s across the two (Table~\ref{tab:tb7}). The in-node figure is
timed per call on the cluster's first node; Table~\ref{tab:tb8}'s 98\,GiB/s is the slope
inside a running program on another A100 node, and each is used where it was measured.

Splitting the same eight devices across the boundary (\texttt{1x8} to \texttt{2x4})
adds 107--110\,ms to B's generation at width 2048, which is more than the 21--51\,ms B
was saving there. So the seed arm, which wins with the all-reduce on one node, loses with
it across two, at both populations and at sixteen devices. At width 512 the all-reduce is
6\,MiB and the seed arm keeps it. Rank 1 favoured replicating already and does so by more.
The dependence on the fabric is the $T_{\mathrm{ar}}$ term of Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} and
nothing new: with the measured terms the equation predicts every \texttt{2x4} cell
within 6\,ms and gets all six \texttt{2x8} signs.

The prediction we fixed before the \texttt{2x8} run did worse, 4 of 6 signs and a penalty
of about 10\,ms instead of 110, because the calibration that fed it all-reduced $1/D$ of
the payload it was labelled with and so reported a bandwidth $D$ times too high
(515 and 9.2\,GiB/s instead of 64 and 0.58). Table~\ref{tab:tb7} shows both. The fabric we
measured is far slower than a production InfiniBand cluster at 12--50\,GiB/s, where a
96\,MiB all-reduce takes 2--8\,ms; by Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} the seed arm would keep the
all-reduce there. That is a prediction, not a measurement.

\subsection{What low-rank noise saves}

% F4 -- experiments/phase2/figures/f4-cost-bfloat16.png (plot_cost.py)
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{f4-cost-bfloat16.png}
  \caption{Time per generation against the dense baseline at the same shape, one device,
    bfloat16. Blue: faster than dense; red: slower. Hatched: the baseline (``dense
    OOM'') or both arms do not fit the device. Ranks 4 and 16 behave like rank 1
    (Table~\ref{tab:tb3}).}
  \label{fig:f4}
\end{figure*}

EGGROLL motivates low-rank noise by what dense perturbations cost on GPUs, so the TPU,
whose matrix unit makes dense multiplies cheap, might narrow the gap. It widens it
(Figure~\ref{fig:f4}). A rank-1 generation costs 0.05--0.16$\times$ the dense one on the
v5e against 0.13--0.42$\times$ on the A100, and on the 16\,GB chip the dense baseline does
not fit over most of the grid. Seed regeneration is the price side of Qiu et al.'s
design: 5.1--5.9$\times$ the dense time on the v5e and 1.3--3.2$\times$ on the A100, for
the only arm that never runs out of memory. Whether that cost is random-number throughput
or the loss of the shared base multiply, we did not isolate.

Forcing full-precision multiplies costs the low-rank arms 1.6--1.9$\times$ and the others
at most 8\% (Table~\ref{tab:tb3}): the low-rank correction multiplies are what gets
upgraded. Computing in bfloat16 rather than float32 changes little, within 7\% on the
v5e, and on the A100 bfloat16 is 8--12\% \emph{slower} for dense and seed noise and
level for rank 1; the datatype is a memory choice more than a speed one.

\subsection{Against the reference code}

\input{generated/tb1}

On one device the rank-1 arm runs at 0.97--1.02$\times$ the throughput of EGGROLL's
released code on the A100 and 0.68--0.85$\times$ on the v5e (Table~\ref{tab:tb1}), a
general API against a specialized one; we did not isolate the TPU gap. The reference
implementations do not shard, so on eight devices the library reaches 1.3--3.4$\times$
the EGGROLL code's throughput on the A100 and 1.7--6.1$\times$ on the v5e. The seed arm
runs at 0.13--0.77$\times$ the naive baseline at these shapes: regeneration costs
throughput, and what it buys is memory. evosax does not fit $d{=}2048$ on the 16\,GB chip.
The reference's rank-1 path also fails to compile on that chip at $d{=}512$,
$N{=}16384$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:refimpl}). We did not benchmark Qiu et al.'s released code.
