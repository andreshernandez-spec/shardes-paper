\subsection{Placement on the block}
\label{subsec:block}

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{f2b-crossover-vs-d.png}
  \caption{Split-update time divided by replicated-update time on the block benchmark.
    Below 1, splitting is faster; above 1, replication is faster. Panels separate hardware
    and matrix width. Marker shape identifies population size. Bars show the range from
    fastest split / slowest replicated repeat to slowest split / fastest replicated
    repeat, across five repeats per placement; these are not confidence intervals.
    The three main variants measured on both platforms are shown; the complete grids,
    including pairing variants, are in Appendix~\ref{sec:grids}.}
  \label{fig:f2}

At eight devices in one host, splitting the update is faster for every full-rank
configuration. Replication is faster for rank-1 perturbations at width 2048
(Figure~\ref{fig:f2}). For those low-rank configurations, splitting takes 4--15\% longer
on the A100 and 17--46\% longer on the v5e. These results include both mirrored and
unpaired sampling; Appendix~\ref{sec:grids} gives all variants and absolute times.

At width 512 the rank-1 placements are close. We treat a comparison as unresolved when
its repeat range contains 1, using the range defined in the figure caption. At eight
devices this occurs in six of the eight low-rank configurations at width 512. Every
full-rank configuration and every low-rank configuration at width 2048 has a repeat
range entirely on one side of 1. These repeats measure variation within a compiled run,
not variation across hardware sessions.

The timing model explains why perturbation type changes the preferred placement.
Reconstructing a full-rank update is expensive, so splitting saves much more time than
its all-reduce adds. With low-rank perturbations, reconstruction is already cheap and
the communication can dominate. For example, the isolated 96\,MiB all-reduce at width
2048 costs 0.93\,ms on the A100 and 2.01\,ms on the v5e (Table~\ref{tab:tb8}). The
larger communication cost on the v5e is consistent with its larger penalty for splitting
low-rank updates.

Two refinements matter when reconstruction is cheap. Its local share does not generally
run $D$ times faster, and adding communication to the reconstruction probe costs more
than the isolated collective benchmark suggests. Using measured local reconstruction
and the added communication cost, Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover} identifies the faster placement
in 18 of 20 A100 configurations and 15 of 16 v5e configurations at eight devices.
The three disagreements are near-ties at width 512. This is an explanation developed
after the sweeps, not a prospective test. Appendix~\ref{sec:timemodel} defines the
measurements and reports their remaining discrepancies.

    generation, including next-token loss evaluation. Splitting is faster for full-rank
    noise; replication is faster at every measured low-rank configuration. The strips
    below the plots mark configurations that run out of memory (OOM); they are separate
    from the timing axis. Absolute times are in Table~\ref{tab:tb5}.}
  \label{fig:f9}
\end{figure*}

The same placement preference holds on Qwen2.5-0.5B on the TPU
(Figure~\ref{fig:f9}). Splitting becomes more advantageous for full-rank noise as devices
are added. Replication is faster in all 18 low-rank configurations that fit, although
the smallest margin is only 1\%. Each repeat range lies above 1. Increasing the rank or
population reduces replication's advantage: it increases reconstruction work without
increasing the model-sized communication. These three directional expectations were
fixed before this experiment.

The memory boundary requires a separate explanation. Here the low-rank variants batch
all candidates on each device, whereas full-rank noise is regenerated and evaluated one
candidate at a time. Batching improves throughput but increases activation memory.
Consequently, the low-rank configurations run out of memory more often despite their
smaller perturbation representation. Matching the full-rank evaluation batching reproduces
this memory boundary (Appendix~\ref{sec:memory}). The placement comparison keeps batching
fixed; a timing comparison across perturbation types does not isolate the effect of rank.

\subsection{Across a slow connection between hosts}
\label{subsec:boundary}

\input{generated/tb7}

Communication can reverse the full-rank preference. We repeated six configurations on
two A100 nodes connected through TCP sockets; the available InfiniBand connection carried
no NCCL traffic. The effective all-reduce bandwidth was 64\,GiB/s within a node and
0.6--0.8\,GiB/s across the two nodes.

Keeping eight devices but splitting them between hosts adds 107--110\,ms to the
split placement's relative cost at width 2048 (Table~\ref{tab:tb7}). This exceeds the
21--51\,ms it saved on one host, so replication becomes faster for regenerated full-rank
noise. At width 512, the update is only 6\,MiB and splitting remains faster for that
variant. Replication's advantage for rank 1 grows across the slow connection.

Our prediction fixed before the sixteen-device run identified four of six faster
placements. Its bandwidth calibration used an incorrectly labelled message size.
Correcting that error after the runs gives all six preferences, but still overestimates
the full-rank penalty at width 2048 by 46--63\,ms. The original prediction and the
retrospective correction remain side by side in Table~\ref{tab:tb7-audit}.
This experiment establishes the effect of a slow connection, not the performance of a
high-bandwidth multi-host cluster.
