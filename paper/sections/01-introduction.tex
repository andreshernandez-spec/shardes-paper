\section{Introduction}

Evolution strategies (ES) train a network without backpropagation: in each iteration,
called a \emph{generation}, they evaluate a \emph{population} of randomly perturbed
copies of the weights, called \emph{candidates}, and move the weights toward the
candidates that scored well. Their evaluations are independent, so the work of one
generation can be divided across many devices.

The standard account of distributed ES is that devices need to exchange only one number
per candidate, its fitness score \citep{salimans2017}. These scores are enough to
reconstruct the weight update when every device can regenerate the perturbations from
shared random seeds. Every device then repeats the entire reconstruction, which we call
\emph{replication}. Alternatively, each device can reconstruct only the part of the
update that comes from its own candidates, and the devices add these partial updates
with an \emph{all-reduce}, an operation that returns their sum to every device. This
approach, which we call \emph{splitting}, divides the reconstruction work across
devices, but the all-reduce moves a model-sized array.

For example, with four devices and 100 candidates, each device evaluates 25 candidates,
and the devices then share all 100 fitness scores. Under replication, every device
reconstructs the update from all 100 perturbations; under splitting, each device
reconstructs a partial update from its own 25, and an all-reduce sums the four partial
updates. Splitting is faster when the computation it saves outweighs the communication
it adds.

The perturbation design changes this balance. \citet{qiu2025} perturb every weight
independently (full-rank noise) and regenerate the noise from a seed instead of storing
it, so reconstructing the update means generating a model-sized noise array again for
every candidate. EGGROLL \citep{eggroll2025} perturbs each weight matrix by the product
of two thin matrices (low-rank noise), so the update can be reconstructed directly
from those factors. Existing systems reconstruct the complete update without dividing it:
Algorithm 2 of \citet{salimans2017} uses replication, while EGGROLL's vLLM setup
reconstructs the update on one primary device and broadcasts the updated model
\citep[Appendix L.2]{eggroll2025}. None of these papers compares the two \emph{update
placements}, replication and splitting, or compares the wall-clock costs of both
perturbation designs under both placements in one implementation. We measure when
splitting is faster than replication.

We built a JAX \citep{jax2018} library\ifanonymous\else, \textbf{shardes}
\citep{shardes},\fi{} to make this measurement. Every device holds a complete copy of
the model, and the population is divided among the devices. The library supports both
perturbation designs under both placements. Within each perturbation design,
comparisons between placements keep the model and evaluation procedure fixed. We ask
three questions:\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{When is splitting faster than replication?} We first measure eight
    devices in one host, linked by a fast interconnect (NVLink on the A100s, the TPU
    interconnect on the v5e). There, splitting is faster for every full-rank configuration
    of a single transformer block, and replication is faster for rank-1 noise at width 2048.
    A timing
    model that compares the reconstruction time saved with the communication time added
    agrees with all 30 configurations in which the two placements are clearly separated
    by repeated measurements.
  \item \textbf{Does the result hold for a real model and across hosts?} On
    Qwen2.5-0.5B on the TPU, the faster placement is the same as on the block: splitting
    for full rank, replication for low rank. Across a slow connection between two A100
    hosts, replication is faster for both perturbation types at width 2048, reversing
    the full-rank result.
  \item \textbf{What do low-rank perturbations save?} On the block benchmark with one
    device, a rank-1 generation takes \RankOneOverDenseGPU{} times as long as one with stored
    full-rank noise on the A100, and \RankOneOverDenseTPU{} times as long on the v5e. These are geometric means of the time ratios over
    matched configurations.
\end{itemize}
