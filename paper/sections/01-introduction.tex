\section{Introduction}

Evolution strategies train a network without gradients: perturb the weights with
random noise, score each perturbed copy, and move the weights toward the copies that
scored well. Two recent papers made the method credible for language models, with
opposite designs. \citet{qiu2025} perturb every weight and avoid storing the noise by
regenerating it from a random seed whenever it is needed. EGGROLL
\citep{eggroll2025} perturbs each weight matrix with the product of two thin
matrices, a rank-$r$ perturbation, so the per-copy work becomes a small correction on
top of one shared matrix multiply.

ES is usually described as cheap to distribute because workers exchange scalar
fitnesses, not gradients \citep{salimans2017}. That describes one way of computing the
update, the weighted sum $\sum_i w_i\epsilon_i$ over the population. If every device
gathers all $N$ fitnesses and recomputes the whole sum, only scalars move. The other
way is for each device to sum its own share of the population and all-reduce the
partial sums, a buffer the size of the model. The second moves far more bytes, but
does $1/D$ of the work on $D$ devices. Which is faster depends on how long the sum
takes against how long the all-reduce takes (Section~\ref{sec:placement},
Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover}). The two published designs sit at opposite ends of that
trade: regenerating full-rank noise makes the sum expensive, and low-rank noise makes
it cheap. GPUs and TPUs sit at different points on the other side, the cost of the
all-reduce. As far as we know, neither side has been measured.

Measuring it needs both designs in one implementation, so that the comparison is
between the placements and not between codebases. The standard JAX ES library,
evosax \citep{lange2022evosax}, stores the population as one dense (population,
parameters) matrix, which rules out never-materialized low-rank noise and does not fit
full-rank noise for large models. We therefore built \textbf{shardes}, a JAX
\citep{jax2018} library that splits the population across devices and takes the
perturbation scheme as a plug-in. The paper is about what it measures.

We also check that the cheaper perturbation is worth having: whether low-rank updates
point in useful directions (their cosine with the exact gradient of a real model), and
whether they train as well on a task.

\paragraph{Contributions, most important first.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Where to compute the update.} All-reduce wins every full-rank configuration on
    one A100 node and one v5e host, and replicating wins for rank 1 at width 2048; the
    signs hold on Qwen2.5-0.5B, and across two TCP-connected nodes the full-rank arm
    flips as well. Eq.~\ref{eq:crossover}, with its terms timed separately, accounts
    for all of it and predicts the host boundary within 6\,ms per cell.
  \item \textbf{What low-rank noise saves, per accelerator.} The low-rank advantage
    over dense noise is larger on the TPU than on the GPU, not smaller.
  \item \textbf{One library for both designs.} The rank-1 path runs within 3\% of
    EGGROLL's released code on one A100 (0.68--0.85$\times$ on one v5e chip), and
    scales across devices, which none of the reference implementations does.
  \item \textbf{The cheap perturbation is not a worse one.} At equal population,
    low-rank updates align with the exact gradient 0.92 to 1.31 times as well as
    full-rank ones at 0.5B and 1.5B parameters. On Countdown every rank reaches the same
    held-out accuracy; full rank gets there in fewer samples, and rank 1 updates
    1.44$\times$ faster.
\end{itemize}
